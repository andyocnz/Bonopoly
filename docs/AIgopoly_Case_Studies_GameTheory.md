# AIgopoly: Game Theory Case Studies

**Real examples of strategic dynamics that emerge during gameplay**

These scenarios illustrate how AIgopoly creates authentic game theory learning moments. Students discover these dynamics through play—parameters are hidden, forcing hypothesis-testing and strategic thinking.

## Case Study 1: The OPEC Trap (Oil & Energy)

### Concept: Cartel Dynamics & Prisoner's Dilemma

**What students see:**
> Round context: "Global oil oversupply. Commodity prices under pressure. Asian buyers are shopping aggressively for the lowest cost."

**What happened:**

| Round | Event |
|-------|-------|
| Round 1 | Prices collapse as everyone undercuts. All teams bleeding margin. |
| Round 2 | Between rounds, teams start talking: "Let's all hold at $95—nobody undercuts." |
| Round 3 | Four teams hold. Team B secretly drops to $88. Team B wins big. |
| Round 4 | Trust shattered. Everyone retaliates. Full price war. |
| Round 5 | All teams barely profitable. Team B's short-term gain erased by retaliation. |

**The learning moment:**
> "We had a deal. Team B broke it. Then we all lost. How do real cartels survive this?"

**Game theory concepts:**
- Prisoner's Dilemma (cooperate vs. defect)
- Cartel instability without enforcement
- Repeated games and reputation
- Tit-for-tat retaliation
- Why OPEC struggles (real-world parallel)

**Debrief questions:**
- Why did Team B defect? Was it rational?
- What would have made the agreement stable?
- How does OPEC try to solve this in real life?

**Pitch line:**
> "Students naturally form cartels in the Oil scenario. Then someone defects. The debrief runs itself—they've lived the prisoner's dilemma."

## Case Study 2: First Mover vs. Fast Follower (Automotive)

### Concept: Commitment, Timing & Irreversible Decisions

**What students see:**
> Round context: "Electric vehicle demand growing. Analysts predict Asia will be the largest EV market within 3 years. Factory construction takes one full round."

**What happened:**

| Round | Team A (First Mover) | Team B (Wait & See) | Team C (Over-commit) |
|-------|---------------------|---------------------|----------------------|
| Round 1 | Commits $1B to Asia factory | Holds cash, watches | Builds 2 factories |
| Round 2 | Under construction | Sees A's move, starts build | Under construction |
| Round 3 | Factory online, captures Asia | Still building | Both online |
| Round 4 | Dominant share, high margin | Finally competing, but behind | Overcapacity, bleeding cash |
| Round 5 | Winner | Viable but trailing | Struggling with debt |

**The learning moment:**
> "We waited for certainty. By the time we knew Asia was hot, Team A was already there. But Team C shows you can also move too fast."

**Game theory concepts:**
- First-mover advantage
- Option value of waiting vs. commitment value
- Sunk costs and irreversibility
- Capacity as a strategic signal
- Goldilocks problem (too early / too late / just right)

**Debrief questions:**
- When is it better to commit early vs. wait for information?
- How did the 1-round construction lag change the game?
- What signals could Team B have read earlier?

**Pitch line:**
> "Automotive teaches capital commitment. $1.5 billion decisions with one-round lag. Students feel the weight of irreversible choices."

## Case Study 3: Cracking the Regional Code (Electronics)

### Concept: Market Segmentation & Information Discovery

**What students see:**
> Round context: "European consumers increasingly value ethical sourcing and sustainability credentials. Asian markets remain fiercely competitive on price. US consumers balance features with affordability."

**What happened:**

| Round | Most Teams | Team C |
|-------|-----------|--------|
| Trial | Optimize for Asia (biggest volume) | Experiments with eco-supplier in Europe |
| Round 1 | Low price, basic features everywhere | Notices Europe margin held up—doubles down |
| Round 2 | Fighting for Asia scraps | Quietly dominating Europe |
| Round 3 | "Why is Team C profitable?" | Expanding Europe focus |
| Round 4 | Try to pivot to Europe—too late | Established position, brand strength |
| Round 5 | Realize they missed the signal | Highest share price |

**The learning moment:**
> "The hints were in the round context all along. Team C actually read them. We just assumed biggest market = best market."

**Game theory concepts:**
- Information asymmetry (same info, different interpretation)
- Exploration vs. exploitation
- Market segmentation strategy
- First-mover advantage in niche markets
- Value of the trial round as reconnaissance

**Debrief questions:**
- What signals did Team C read that others missed?
- Why is the trial round valuable even though it "doesn't count"?
- How do real companies discover underserved segments?

**Pitch line:**
> "Students get the same context clues. Some read them, some don't. The ones who experiment in the trial round find edges others miss."

## Case Study 4: The R&D Arms Race (Electronics)

### Concept: Diminishing Returns & Opportunity Cost

**What students see:**
> Round context: "Consumers excited about new features. Tech reviewers praise innovation. However, analysts note feature fatigue in mature markets."

**What happened:**

| Round | Team A & B (Arms Race) | Team D (Lean Strategy) |
|-------|----------------------|----------------------|
| Round 1 | Pour resources into R&D | Minimal R&D, invests in capacity |
| Round 2 | Racing to unlock features | 2 basic features, low price |
| Round 3 | 8 features each, costs escalating | 3 features, strong Asia share |
| Round 4 | 12 features—but margins thin | Holds position, healthy profit |
| Round 5 | Most features, but ROI poor | Fewer features, best profitability |

**Post-game analysis:**
> Team A & B unlocked the most features, but each new feature cost exponentially more research hours. Team D realized "good enough" beats "best" if the cost is too high.

**The learning moment:**
> "We won the feature race but lost the profit race. Features 8-12 cost us more than they were worth."

**Game theory concepts:**
- Diminishing marginal returns
- Opportunity cost (R&D hours vs. other investments)
- Arms race dynamics (competitive escalation)
- "Good enough" strategy
- When to stop competing on one dimension

**Debrief questions:**
- At what point did additional features stop paying off?
- How do you know when you're in an arms race?
- What's the real-world parallel? (Smartphone features? Streaming content wars?)

**Pitch line:**
> "Two teams race to have the most features. A third team stays lean and wins on profit. Classic diminishing returns—students discover it themselves."

## Case Study 5: The Sustainability Pivot (Oil & Energy)

### Concept: Differentiation in Commodity Markets

**What students see:**
> Round 1: "Oil prices stable. Standard commodity dynamics."
> Round 3: "European regulators announce carbon pricing. ESG investors pressuring boards."
> Round 5: "Carbon border adjustments coming. Green premiums emerging in Europe."

**What happened:**

| Round | Teams A-C (Commodity Play) | Team D (Green Pivot) |
|-------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| Round 1-2 | Price competition, thin margins | Same—surviving |
| Round 3 | Sees regulation news, ignores it | Invests in Carbon Capture feature |
| Round 4 | Still competing on price | Unlocks Renewables Portfolio |
| Round 5 | Crushed by carbon costs | Premium pricing in Europe, tax breaks |

**The learning moment:**
> "We thought oil is oil—commodity, lowest cost wins. Team D saw the regulation shift and escaped the trap."

**Game theory concepts:**
- Escaping prisoner's dilemma through differentiation
- Reading signals / scenario shifts
- Commodity trap vs. value creation
- Regulatory risk as strategic factor
- Real-world ESG transition parallel

**Debrief questions:**
- How did Team D know to pivot before others?
- Is this "cheating" the commodity game or smart strategy?
- What real oil companies are doing this? (BP, Shell, etc.)

**Pitch line:**
> "In commodity markets, price war seems inevitable. But the scenario context signals a sustainability shift. Teams that read it escape the trap."

## Case Study 6: The Bluff (Any Industry)

### Concept: Signaling, Deception & Incomplete Information

**What students see:**
> Between rounds, teams can observe some competitor metrics: market share, capacity expansion announcements, feature launches.

**What happened:**

| Round | Team A | Other Teams |
|-------|--------|-------------|
| Round 2 | Announces "major Asia expansion" loudly | Worried—scale back Asia plans |
| Round 3 | Actually builds small capacity | Others ceded Asia share |
| Round 4 | Dominates Asia at low cost | "Wait, they didn't actually expand much?" |
| Round 5 | Wins through bluff | Others overreacted to signal |

**The learning moment:**
> "They announced expansion. We believed them and backed off. They barely built anything. We got played."

**Game theory concepts:**
- Signaling and credible commitment
- Bluffing and strategic deception
- Information asymmetry
- Reaction to competitor announcements
- When to believe signals vs. verify

**Debrief questions:**
- Was Team A's bluff ethical? Effective?
- How could others have verified the signal?
- When are announcements credible?

**Pitch line:**
> "Students learn that competitor announcements aren't always true. Signals can be strategic. Trust but verify."
